A practical training guide

How to type faster—with a plan you can actually follow.

Start by finding where your typing stops flowing: searching for keys, correcting mistakes, pausing at punctuation, or losing your place. Practice that specific problem, return to full sentences, then check whether the improvement carries over.

1. Establish a baseline before changing your technique

Take three runs of the English typing test using the same duration, keyboard and passage mode. Pause between runs and keep all results. Use the middle WPM score as a simple baseline, alongside the accuracy and error counts.

During the runs, notice what interrupts you. Are you looking down to find letters? Do you repeatedly reverse the same pair? Does a capital letter make you stop? A low score identifies a result; the interruption identifies something you can practice.

Set one goal for the next session. For example: “Type words containing R without looking down,” or “Use capitals without stopping before Shift.” Keep your regular test conditions consistent while you experiment with the drill.

If a score is confusing, read how WPM and accuracy work. In TypingEase, corrected mistakes can disappear from the final accuracy result, so a high percentage should be considered alongside how often you had to correct.

Do not change your keyboard, layout, technique and test mode all at once. If everything changes, it becomes hard to tell which adjustment helped or created a new difficulty.

3. Make key finding predictable

If you are learning English QWERTY touch typing, use the home row as your starting reference: left fingers on A, S, D and F; right fingers on J, K, L and semicolon. The raised marks on F and J help you locate the index-finger positions without looking.

Practice reaching for a key and recovering a comfortable position. You do not need to force every finger onto the home row after every letter or keep your hands rigid. The goal is to know where the next key is and reach it consistently, without searching.

Use a repeatable finger choice for a key while learning. When a reach is unfamiliar, briefly check the position, look back at the text and try it again slowly. Gradually reduce those checks. Covering the keyboard is optional; it is not helpful if you simply become stuck and tense.

The touch-typing guide explains the basic positions, and the English course introduces keys in sequence. Use the course for your selected layout; QWERTY finger positions should not be copied blindly onto a different layout.

If you already type comfortably with your own established method, first investigate its actual limitations. A technique change is useful when it solves a recurring problem. Expect your score to be temporarily different while learning a new movement; measure the new skill separately from your old personal best.

4. Practice the transition, then put it back into a sentence

A useful drill is narrow enough that you can observe a change. Select one letter, pair or symbol that repeatedly interrupted your baseline. Start slowly, then vary the surrounding text so you are learning the movement rather than memorizing one string.

Drill A: a hesitant letter

If R is the problem, begin with a few controlled F-to-R reaches on QWERTY. Then type red river read around. Finish with the sentence below. Check that you can find R from different preceding letters, not only from a resting position.

Read the report near the river.

Drill B: a reversed pair

If you often type “teh” instead of “the,” slow down the H-to-E transition. Practice the then them there, with a brief pause between words if needed. Finish by copying a fresh sentence containing several of them.

Put the notes there, then read them.

Once the sequence feels predictable, remove the deliberate pause and keep a gentle rhythm. If the reversal returns, lower the pace again. Repeating an error faster is not the purpose of the drill.

Drill C: capitals and punctuation

Practice a capital with the opposite-hand Shift key where comfortable, then release Shift before the following lowercase letters. Include the punctuation you actually use. Here is an original practice sentence with capitals, a comma, an apostrophe and a question mark:

On Friday, can Maya check Sam's notes?

Use a similar sentence with a date or number if those are your sticking points. A symbol drill should match your keyboard layout and real work; symbols can occupy different keys across layouts.

Check for transfer

After a few minutes, leave the drill and type an unfamiliar paragraph. Ask whether the same movement is easier there. If only the memorized drill improves, add more varied words and sentences before increasing speed.

5. Increase the pace in small experiments

Start at a speed that allows a clean, controlled passage. As a practical guardrail, aim for roughly 98–99% displayed accuracy with few corrections, then try a slightly quicker run. This is a suggested practice target, not a scientific cutoff or a requirement for every learner.

Alternate a comfortable passage with a modestly faster one. If the faster run creates repeated mistakes or long correction pauses, return to the comfortable pace and identify the specific sequence that broke down. If you can repeat the faster pace on fresh text with similar accuracy, use it as your new working pace.

Do not force an identical interval between every key. Some words and symbols need more attention. Aim to reduce unnecessary stops while leaving enough time to read and enter the next part correctly.

Look at the text you are copying and allow your eyes to take in the next short group of words when you can. Avoid racing far ahead if it makes you omit words. The point is to prepare the next movement without losing your place.

6. Use a short, structured practice session

The following ten-minute routine is a starting template. Shorten it if concentration or comfort drops; extend practice only when it stays useful. The timings are editorial suggestions, not a proven optimal dose.

A suggested ten-minute session
TimeTask
2 minutesWarm up on familiar words at an easy pace. Settle your hands and notice tension.
3 minutesWork on one weak letter, transition or symbol using a focused drill.
3 minutesType full sentences that include the skill, followed by unfamiliar text.
1 minuteTake a short check using your usual test conditions.
1 minuteRecord the result and choose the next session's focus. Rest your hands.

Your note can be brief: “R easier without looking; still pausing before capitals; 46 WPM with two remaining errors.” That is more useful for planning the next session than repeating the test until a best score appears.

A two-week plan you can adapt

This plan organizes practice; it does not promise a particular WPM increase in fourteen days. Move a session or repeat a stage when needed. Beginners may spend longer on key positions, while experienced typists may start with punctuation or consistency.

  1. Days 1–2: measure and observe. Establish your three-run baseline. Identify one frequent interruption and review the relevant key positions.
  2. Days 3–4: isolate the problem. Use a focused drill, then several varied words. Finish with a short unfamiliar sentence to check transfer.
  3. Day 5: add normal punctuation. Keep the same skill in sentences with capitals and punctuation. Slow down enough to complete the text reliably.
  4. Day 6: integrate. Practice a fresh paragraph at a comfortable pace. Notice whether you look down or correct less often.
  5. Day 7: rest or review. Read your notes and choose whether to keep the same focus. There is no need to force a test on a tired day.
  6. Days 8–9: address the next bottleneck. Keep a little practice on the first skill, then target the next recurring hesitation.
  7. Days 10–11: test a higher pace. Alternate controlled and slightly faster passages. Back off if accuracy or comfort deteriorates.
  8. Day 12: match real use. Type text similar to your work: an email, notes, names and dates, or code symbols. Keep private material out of shared tools.
  9. Day 13: practice lightly. Review the movements that still need attention. Avoid turning the session into repeated maximum-speed attempts.
  10. Day 14: remeasure. Repeat the original test setup with three fresh, comparable passages. Compare typical speed, accuracy, corrections and comfort, then choose the next focus.

If your WPM is unchanged but you look down less or complete the same task with fewer interruptions, record that improvement. If nothing changes, revise the drill rather than automatically making sessions longer.

What to change when progress stalls

You are practicing what you already do well

Easy word lists can feel productive because the score is high. Review your actual mistakes and spend part of the session on a less comfortable transition. Keep the challenge small enough to complete accurately.

You are measuring a moving target

A new keyboard or harder passage can hide improvement. Return to the same benchmark conditions for your progress check, while recording other practice modes separately.

You are using the personal best as your baseline

A record is one successful attempt. Compare typical results over several runs. Making a formerly rare speed repeatable is progress even without a new record.

You are tired, tense or rushing

Pause and come back later. Place the keyboard in front of you, keep shoulders relaxed and wrists aligned with the forearms, and avoid pressing your wrists against a hard edge. OSHA's keyboard guidance discusses workstation placement and short breaks. Stop a practice session if it causes pain.

You are expecting a drill to replace all real typing

A drill simplifies one problem; actual writing combines many. Keep full sentences in the routine and include the kind of text you need outside the test.

Common questions about getting faster

How long does it take to increase typing speed?

There is no reliable timeline for an individual. Your starting habits, task, consistency and practice quality all matter. Use the two-week plan as a review cycle, then adjust it from your results.

Should I buy a mechanical keyboard?

A purchase is not a prerequisite for these exercises. First check whether your current keyboard is comfortable, reliable and suitable for your layout. A different device may require an adjustment period and is not a guaranteed speed improvement.

Should I correct mistakes while practicing?

Follow the drill or test rules. In ordinary passage practice, correcting the first mismatch helps restore alignment and reinforces the intended sequence. Notice repeated errors afterward so the next session can target them.

Will typing games help?

Typing games can add variety and give you another reason to practice. Check whether the game exercises your current weak point, and retain some sentence practice so you can judge transfer to normal typing.

What should I do after reaching my WPM goal?

Check whether the skill serves your actual tasks. You might maintain the pace, improve punctuation or endurance, or spend your time on editing and useful shortcuts instead of continually raising the target.

Touch typing: finger placement and practice without looking down

Touch typing means finding keys through familiar movements rather than searching for every letter with your eyes. The useful part is being able to watch what you are writing. You do not need a particular speed before you start, and you do not need to erase every habit you already have. This guide uses the English QWERTY letter layout. Try the movements slowly, then use the lesson player to practise them with feedback.

Start with the moment that interrupts you

Before changing your technique, type a short sentence you know. Notice where you pause. Do you look down for almost every letter, lose your place after reaching for a number, or stop only at capitals? Those are different problems. Someone still finding F and J needs a smaller exercise than someone who can type sentences but hesitates at quotation marks.

Also notice what you are doing with the other hand. If both hands travel across the keyboard to find the same letters, a consistent division of keys gives you something concrete to practise. If your existing method already feels reliable, you can work on a particular weakness without declaring the whole method wrong. The purpose of practice is to make writing easier, not to pass a test of how a typist should look.

Choose one observation for today's session. “Return to F after reaching up” is useful because you can see whether you did it. “Become a fast typist” is too broad to guide your next keystroke.

Find a starting position you can return to

On a QWERTY keyboard, place the left little, ring, middle and index fingers over A, S, D and F. Place the right index, middle, ring and little fingers over J, K, L and the semicolon key. Let a thumb reach the space bar. The small raised marks commonly found on F and J help you locate the starting position without inspecting every key.

These positions are a reference, not an instruction to press eight keys or hold your hands rigidly. Begin by finding the two index-finger positions, then settle the remaining fingers alongside them. If your keyboard has a different layout, match the exercise to that layout before following a QWERTY diagram. The letter printed beside a familiar physical position may be different.

Press F once, release it, then press J. Keep the movement small enough that you can tell which finger moved. Add a space between pairs. If you have to look down, use the glance to check one specific position, then look back at the text. There is no benefit in guessing repeatedly while your hands are misplaced.

Reach for one new key, then recover

When a lesson adds a key, first identify which finger the on-screen keyboard assigns to it. Practise the movement from the starting position to that key and back. Do this separately from trying to type a whole word. You are learning both the destination and how to recover your position afterwards.

For example, on QWERTY the left index finger reaches from F towards R. A short sequence alternating the two can expose whether your whole hand is drifting. If the next home-row letter keeps coming out wrong, stop and find F again before continuing. Adding more letters while the starting position is uncertain gives you more things to troubleshoot at once.

Give capitals their own practice

A capital introduces another movement: holding Shift while pressing a letter. Work on a small set of familiar letters first. Where practical, use the Shift key on the opposite side from the letter you are typing, then release it before the next lowercase letter. Watch the result rather than assuming the key combination worked.

Do not add capitals, numbers and unfamiliar punctuation in the same exercise merely to make it harder. If a sentence breaks down at every comma, isolate commas with familiar words. Once that movement is dependable, return to the sentence. Difficulty is useful when it tells you what to change.

Try a small exercise and inspect what happened

Here is an illustrative drill, not a timed challenge or a sample of somebody's results: f j f j ff jj fj jf. Read the next group before typing it. Use the index fingers for F and J, and a thumb for the spaces. Keep your eyes near the text so you can compare what you intended with what appeared.

After one line, ask three questions. Did I use the same finger each time? Did my hands stay near their starting position? Did I notice a wrong letter immediately? Repeat only the part that gave you trouble. If fj felt straightforward but jf caused a pause, practise the second transition instead of restarting the whole exercise automatically.

Next, add one new key from your current lesson. Keep most of the exercise familiar so you can identify the effect of that change. This is also a useful way to approach punctuation later: an unfamiliar movement becomes easier to inspect when it sits among movements you already understand.

The typing course provides a sequence of lessons if you would rather not assemble exercises yourself. Start with the first available lesson when the home keys are unfamiliar. If you already know them, use the lesson descriptions to identify the section that matches your difficulty and check which units are available in your current progress.

When looking down keeps coming back

You look down at the start of every word

Reduce the amount you read ahead. Try a short group of letters with your hands already positioned, rather than a long sentence that asks you to remember the text and find the keys together. Look down deliberately to locate a missing key, then repeat that movement while looking at the screen. The goal is to reduce unnecessary searching, not to make a necessary check feel like failure.

Your hands move after a long reach

Pause after the reach and find the home position again. If the following letter is wrong, the problem may be the return movement rather than the new key itself. Practise that pair of movements before adding speed. A useful note is “lose position after P,” because it gives you a precise place to restart tomorrow.

You rush through easy words and stumble at difficult ones

Use an exercise that repeats the troublesome transition with a few different familiar neighbours. Avoid treating the easy part as a sprint that compensates for mistakes elsewhere. A typing score describes an attempt; it does not tell you whether each movement was deliberate. You can complete a useful session at a slower pace if it exposes and improves the part that was interrupting you.

Why we prefer intentional habits from the beginning

The approach behind TypingEase is to offer guidance when someone first starts using a keyboard regularly. Finding the right letter is an understandable first goal. We want to add a little structure at that point: where to start, how to choose a finger, and how to return attention to the screen. That is an editorial preference about teaching, not a promise that one age group learns faster.

For a child beginning to write schoolwork or search for information on a computer, familiarity with the keyboard can be a useful preparation for those tasks. The aim is independence, not winning a speed contest. A short, understandable exercise is a better starting point than an adult performance target. This guide does not establish an ideal starting age or claim that TypingEase is a specialist children's curriculum.

Adults are not late to the process. If you have years of typing behind you, begin with the particular habit you want to change. Keep the parts that already serve you, and give a new movement enough attention to decide whether it helps.

Questions you may have before practising

Should I use all ten fingers immediately?

Start with the fingers needed by the current exercise. A course can introduce movements progressively; trying to use every finger on a full paragraph on day one can obscure what you are learning. “Ten-finger typing” describes an approach to distributing work, not a reason to ignore your own physical circumstances. Adapt the task where needed rather than forcing a movement to match a diagram.

How long should a session last?

Choose a session you can finish while still paying attention to the movement you selected. One carefully observed exercise is a reasonable start. Add more when you can still explain what you are practising. There is no completion date promised here: prior experience, keyboard familiarity and the difficulty of the material all affect how practice feels.

Should I take a speed test after every lesson?

You can, but it is not necessary to turn every lesson into a comparison. Use a typing test when you want a broader snapshot, and compare similar language, duration and text conditions. During a technique exercise, observe the specific movement first. Read how WPM is calculated before treating scores from different tools as interchangeable.

Will this transfer to a different keyboard?

Start with a few familiar characters and check the layout. Key sizes and the positions of some punctuation keys can differ, even when the letter arrangement looks familiar. Find your starting position again before judging a slower attempt. If you want to compare practice results, using the same keyboard removes one source of variation. Learning to adapt to another device can be a separate, useful task.

What if I type well with fewer fingers?

You do not need to discard a working method simply because it differs from the diagram. Decide what you want to improve: fewer downward glances, steadier punctuation, or easier use of both hands. Try a focused change and judge it on that task. This guide offers a starting structure, not proof that every self-taught typist is slow.

Choose the next movement

Return to the observation you made at the beginning. If you are still finding letter positions, open a beginner lesson. If the letters are comfortable but sentences break down, pick the relevant section of the course roadmap. Leave yourself one short note about the movement to revisit. That note is more useful for the next session than a demand to beat today's score.

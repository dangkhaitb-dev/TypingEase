A beginner's guide

Touch typing: how to type without looking

Each finger owns a small patch of the keyboard, and your hands come back to the same eight keys after every reach. That is the whole idea. This page covers what to learn, in what order, and how long it honestly takes.

Touch typing is typing by feel. Eight fingers rest on a fixed row of keys, each one is responsible for the keys immediately around it, and after every press the finger returns to where it started. Your eyes stay on the screen the entire time.

Hunt-and-peck works too, and some people are quick at it. The limit is not in the fingers. It is that every key you have to find is a key you have to look at.

Why looking down is the thing that caps you

Two costs, and the second is the bigger one.

The first is the round trip. Keyboard, screen, keyboard, screen — each switch is short, and there are thousands of them in an afternoon.

The second is that while you are looking at your hands, you are not reading what you are typing. Mistakes go unnoticed until you look up, so you find them a sentence late instead of at the keystroke that made them. Then you have to reread, find the spot, and fix it.

There is also the part that never improves. Hunting for a key is a visual search, and a visual search stays roughly as slow in year ten as in year one. A remembered movement does not: repeat it enough and it stops needing your attention at all. That is the difference the home row is buying you.

Speeds are quoted in words per minute, where a "word" means five characters. Published averages vary widely with who was measured and what they were asked to type, so treat any single figure with suspicion. As rough anchors: something near 40 wpm is an ordinary adult speed, 60–80 wpm is a comfortable touch typist, and sustained speeds above 100 wpm are uncommon. The lessons here count only the characters you got right, so the number on screen is already net of your mistakes.

The home row, and which finger owns which key

Home position is the middle row of letters. Rest your fingertips here and leave them here:

A  S  D  F J  K  L  ;

Left hand

  • Index: F
  • Middle: D
  • Ring: S
  • Little: A

Right hand

  • Index: J
  • Middle: K
  • Ring: L
  • Little: ;

F and J carry a small raised bump. That bump is the whole reason touch typing is learnable: it lets both index fingers find home position by feel, in the dark, without a glance. Slide a finger along the middle row until you feel it, let the other three fall onto the keys beside it, and you are set.

The space bar belongs to your right thumb, which already rests on it and presses from where it is — no lifting, no aiming.

Three more keys belong to this row without sitting under a finger at rest. The index fingers stretch inward for G and H. And the right little finger reaches one key past the semicolon for the apostrophe, which in English earns its place faster than any other punctuation mark: without it there is no don't, no it's, no you're.

The rule that matters: a finger leaves home, presses one key, and comes straight back. If you have to hunt for the next key, your hands have drifted — find the bumps again.

Posture and hands

  • Sit back in the chair with your feet flat on the floor. Elbows near your sides, forearms roughly level with the keyboard.
  • Wrists straight and floating, not parked on the desk edge. Resting them there forces the fingers to reach at an angle.
  • Fingers curved, as if holding a small ball. Tap with the fingertip, not the flat of the finger.
  • Screen far enough away that you are not leaning in to read it. Shoulders down.

None of this needs a special chair or an expensive keyboard. The one thing worth checking: if your wrists have to bend upward to reach the keys, the keyboard is too high or the chair is too low. Fix that before anything else.

Learn the keys in an order, not all at once

Two new keys at a time, and the order follows the hand rather than the alphabet: index finger, middle, ring, little finger across the home row, then the same stretch mirrored onto the row above and the row below. Both hands learn the same movement at the same moment, which is why the second key of each pair costs almost nothing after the first.

It would be possible to order the keys by how common they are in English instead. It is not worth it. What you are training is the row discipline — leave, press, return — and scattering the keys across three rows on day one breaks exactly that. You lose little by waiting: the first unit already gives you E, A, I, S, R, H, L and D, enough that the drills can use real English words rather than nonsense.

Capitals and Enter come after the alphabet, because Shift is a reach with the little finger of the other hand and it is easier once both hands are steady. The apostrophe waits until lesson 18, late for a home-row key — but it arrives alongside the full stop and comma, at the point where the practice text stops being single words and starts being sentences. Sentences are where you notice its absence.

Numbers, symbols and long paragraphs are last. By then the letters cost you nothing, which is the only reason the number row is bearable.

The mistake that costs the most: speed before accuracy

Almost every beginner does this. You learn six keys, you feel the rhythm arrive, and you push the pace to see what happens. What happens is errors.

A wrong key costs at least three keystrokes — the wrong one, the backspace, the right one — plus the moment it takes to notice. So a run at 90% accuracy is not 10% worse than a clean one; it is considerably worse than that, and it feels worse too, because the flow keeps breaking.

The real cost is quieter. Typing fast and wrong is still practice: you are rehearsing a reach that misses, and your fingers learn the miss along with everything else. Unlearning it later takes longer than getting it right now would have.

That is why the lessons here set an accuracy floor — 70% on the first four, 80% after that — and a screen typed below it scores nothing, however fast it was. It is not a grade. It is there to stop you drilling a mistake.

Speed is not something you add later by trying harder. It is what is left over when the reaches stop needing your attention.

How long it takes

It depends on how much you type in a normal day, so nobody can give you a real number. The rough shape, for short daily sessions:

  • A few sessions before you stop looking down on the home row.
  • Two or three weeks of ten to twenty minutes a day to get through the whole alphabet.
  • A month or two of ordinary everyday typing before touch typing is faster than the method it replaced.

The part nobody warns you about is the dip in the middle. For the first week or two you will be slower than you were before, sometimes much slower, and the old method is right there and still works. That is the whole difficulty of learning to touch type. Almost everyone who gives up gives up here, and the way through it is to keep the sessions short enough that you come back tomorrow.

Short and regular beats long and occasional. Fifteen minutes on five days does more than an hour on one.

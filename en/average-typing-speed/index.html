Average typing speed: what does your WPM really tell you?

There is no single average that represents everyone. One large 2018 online study found about 52 WPM among its volunteers. That is a useful reference—not a universal target or a pass mark for your work.

What the research actually found

Dhakal and colleagues analyzed 168,960 volunteers and reported an average of 51.56 WPM, with a standard deviation of 20.20. Participants copied English sentences in an online test. The sample was self-selected, so the result should not be treated as an average for all adults, every country or every age group. Read the original CHI 2018 study (PDF).

The useful takeaway is that performance varied considerably. A large sample does not remove selection bias, and a sentence-copying test does not measure how quickly someone can plan, write and revise an email.

Use an average to orient yourself, not to grade yourself. Before comparing a score, ask who took the test, what they typed, how long they typed, and how errors were scored.

You may encounter different averages elsewhere. They can describe different groups or test settings rather than contradictory findings. Without a stated source and method, a neat benchmark is difficult to interpret. This guide does not assign percentiles or age norms from an overall average.

What is a good typing speed?

A good speed is one that lets you complete your actual task accurately and comfortably. For a beginner, finding letters without looking down may be the most valuable next step. For someone who already types fluently, punctuation, endurance or fewer corrections may matter more than another short-test record.

The bands below are TypingEase practice guidelines, not population statistics, professional qualifications or validated skill levels. Use the row closest to your repeatable English passage score to choose a focus, and adjust it to your needs.

A person in any band can have strong or weak accuracy. Someone at 35 WPM on demanding technical text is not necessarily less capable than someone at 60 WPM on a memorized list of easy words. Keep the passage type attached to the number.

Is 40 WPM good?

It can be a useful working pace for your tasks, especially if you rarely need to stop and correct. If the keyboard still interrupts your thinking, work on the specific interruptions rather than treating 40 as either a failure or a finish line.

Is 60 WPM good?

A repeatable 60 WPM is a reasonable personal milestone. Its practical value depends on whether you can maintain it with accurate text and the punctuation your work requires. One fast run does not establish your everyday pace.

Is 80 or 100 WPM necessary?

Only if your particular goal requires that pace. Higher transcription speed can be useful, but it does not replace understanding, composition or checking your work. For an assessment, use the published requirement rather than a generic internet target.

Put accuracy next to every speed score

A WPM number without error information is incomplete. In a test that includes incorrect characters in its speed count, rushing through mistakes can raise WPM while making the answer less useful.

Consider two hypothetical one-minute attempts. The first leaves 300 characters with 30 wrong: 60 WPM at 90% final-text accuracy. The second leaves 275 characters with three wrong: 55 WPM at roughly 99% accuracy. The second contains slightly more correct characters—272 versus 270—and far fewer mismatches to repair.

This example does not prove that a slower attempt is always better. It shows why the scoring definition matters. Some assessments already penalize errors in the headline speed; others display them separately. See gross WPM, net WPM and accuracy before comparing them.

For practice, you could use roughly 98–99% accuracy as a starting guardrail and only raise the pace when you can repeat it with few corrections. That is a suggested training rule, not an official standard. If the tool measures the final text, also pay attention to errors you erased; they may disappear from accuracy while still consuming time.

Why your average changes with the test

Common words versus full sentences

A lowercase word list can bypass capitals, apostrophes and sentence punctuation. Full prose adds those demands. If your score falls when punctuation is enabled, identify which symbols cause delays and practice them in context; do not assume your basic letter speed has disappeared.

A short burst versus a longer passage

A brief test can capture a strong start. A longer run asks you to sustain the pace and recover from interruptions. Record duration and compare runs of the same length before interpreting a change.

Familiar versus unfamiliar text

Repeating a passage can make it easier to anticipate. That is useful for a focused drill, but it can make a progress test misleading. Keep a few fresh passages of comparable difficulty for your regular check.

Keyboard, language and input method

A different layout, a compact laptop keyboard, or a second language can change the task. Autocorrect and predictive text introduce another difference. Keep separate baselines when you change these conditions rather than combining all results into one unexplained average.

Copying versus composing

Copying provides the words in advance. Writing an argument, choosing an answer for a customer, or debugging code involves decisions between keystrokes. A typing-test result is not a prediction of finished pages per hour.

Find your own repeatable average

You do not need dozens of tests. Start with a small, consistent sample and record enough context to make next week's comparison fair.

  1. Choose your setup. Use one keyboard, language, duration and passage mode. A 60-second passage test is a convenient starting point.
  2. Warm up briefly. Type a little familiar text at an easy pace without counting it as a result.
  3. Take three measured runs. Pause between them and use fresh text of similar difficulty. Record WPM, accuracy and any repeated stumbling point.
  4. Keep a typical result. Sort the three speeds and take the middle one. Keep the individual results too, so you can see how much they vary.
  5. Repeat after practice. Compare the same conditions, including accuracy, instead of comparing your newest run with your all-time best.
Example: your runs are 46, 52 and 48 WPM. Their arithmetic mean is 48.7 WPM, while the median is 48 WPM. Either can be useful if used consistently. For a simple personal log, we suggest the median; it is less affected by one exceptional run.

If the speeds vary widely, first look for causes: rushing the opening line, unfamiliar symbols, fatigue or repeated corrections. Narrowing that variation can be useful progress even before your typical WPM rises.

A compact log entry could read: “Week 1: median 48 WPM; all three attempts at 98–99% displayed accuracy; frequent pauses before capitals.” That note suggests a practice task. A bare personal-best number does not.

Choose a target that matches the work

Set your next goal around a behavior you can check. “Type a fresh paragraph with fewer corrections” is actionable. “Become above average” does not tell you what to practice.

  • School or everyday writing: practice sentences and punctuation so you can keep your attention on the words you want to write.
  • Customer support or administrative work: practice accurate names, dates, numbers and common phrases. Verify any employer's actual test conditions.
  • Programming: include the symbols and shortcuts you use. A prose score does not measure code quality or problem-solving speed.
  • A recruitment assessment: match its duration, language, allowed corrections and required net or gross score. An informal browser result is not a certification.

A small speed target can help once the conditions are fixed. For example, if you repeatedly score around 45 WPM, try to make 50 repeatable without adding corrections. The five-WPM step is an example, not a promised improvement or prescribed rate of progress.

Common questions about average typing speed

What is the average typing speed for my age?

This guide does not provide age-specific averages because the overall study result cannot establish them. To interpret an age benchmark, you would need a suitable age group, enough participants and comparable test conditions.

Am I slow if I am below the study average?

Your result is below that sample's mean only if the measurement is comparable. It is not a diagnosis of your ability or potential. Your starting experience, text and test setup matter when choosing what to improve.

What WPM should I put on a résumé?

Use a result you can reproduce and describe its conditions, including accuracy and duration. If the employer requests a certified assessment or a particular scoring system, follow that requirement.

Can I compare my phone score with my laptop score?

Keep them separate. The input method, keyboard size and assistance features change what you are measuring. Improvement on one device need not translate directly to the other.

How often should I retest?

A weekly check is a practical starting schedule, with shorter checks when you need feedback on a specific drill. If testing consumes most of your practice time, reduce it and spend more time on the problem you identified.
